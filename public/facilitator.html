Facilitator guide

Educational use · English edition 1.0.0 · Download the 10-page PDF

SECTION 1

Purpose, audience, and limits

This guide supports educational use of the 60-Day Total Mental Makeover with ages 14–24. It is not a clinician manual, diagnostic tool, treatment protocol, or facilitator certification. Use the curriculum within your role, training, and organization’s policies. Do not represent completion as recovery or professional competence.

Before running a group, confirm a suitable age grouping, an accessible setting, a responsible supervisor, current local support contacts, and a written safeguarding and emergency procedure. Youth and adult groups can have different consent and privacy requirements. Arrange review by the organization’s qualified safeguarding lead rather than improvising requirements from this guide.

Participants may pass, pause, use fictional examples, write privately, or choose another accessible format. Never require trauma disclosure, public mood scores, personal testimony, or a dramatic success story. Do not diagnose, recommend medication changes, or promise that the exercises will resolve distress.

Preparation task: write down the purpose of the group, who is responsible for safeguarding, the limits of your role, and the person who can advise when a concern exceeds it. If these are unclear, resolve them before facilitating.

SECTION 2

Set up privacy and participant choice

Explain what the group will do, what is optional, and the limits of confidentiality before activities begin. Do not promise secrecy. Participants should understand that serious safety concerns may require action under the organization’s policy. Peers should be asked to respect privacy, while acknowledging that a facilitator cannot guarantee what others repeat.

The website has no participant account or reporting database. Entries start in page memory; optional local saving uses the same browser profile. It is not encrypted by this workbook. Avoid shared-device storage for sensitive writing. Participants can use fictional examples and should control whether to export, print, or delete their work.

Collect only information needed for your authorized program. Do not ask participants to upload journals or mood histories as evidence of attendance. If your organization keeps attendance or service records, use its approved secure system and retention rules, not a public worksheet.

Opening words: “You choose what to share. You may pass, keep your writing private, or use an imagined situation. If a safety concern arises, I will follow our safeguarding procedure and explain the next step as clearly as possible.”

SECTION 3

A repeatable session rhythm

For a 45–60 minute educational meeting, allow about five minutes for welcome and choice reminders, ten for the selected concept and example, ten for guided practice, ten for optional discussion, and five for a next-step review. Keep additional time for access needs and breaks. This timing is a planning aid, not a requirement.

Select one lesson from the current five-day chapter as the shared focus. Participants can complete other lessons independently or in additional meetings. Read the objective, explain the example in plain language, and use the visual sequence. Ask participants to test understanding with a fictional situation before inviting personal application.

Offer quiet writing, speaking privately, drawing, assistive technology, or mental rehearsal. Do not equate length of writing with engagement. Keep a visible stop or pass option. End by asking what one practical action or support request is appropriate, including choosing to rest.

Debrief questions: What distinction did the example show? What might make this tool useful or unsuitable? What information or support would someone need before acting? Finish with a reminder that no one owes the group a personal account.

SECTION 4

Chapters 1–3: notice, think, and name

Chapter 1, Days 1–5: focus on event versus interpretation, the starting snapshot, thought–feeling–action distinctions, optional grounding, and a flexible learning agreement. Use a late message or a confusing instruction as an example. Ask: What can be observed? What remains unknown? What can the person choose?

Chapter 2, Days 6–10: explore automatic lines, thinking shortcuts, fuller evidence, balanced responses, and safe learning experiments. Avoid using “thinking error” to dismiss a real concern or structural harm. Ask: What observation supports or complicates the conclusion? What is a believable alternative? What low-stakes question could provide information?

Chapter 3, Days 11–15: practice emotional vocabulary, early signals, feelings versus actions, needs and requests, and a review. Let participants say “I do not know” or use broad feeling words. Ask: What kind of support might this person need? How could a specific request be phrased?

Facilitation check: participants can explain the distinctions using a fictional situation without revealing history. If a grounding activity is uncomfortable, stop it. Do not require closed eyes, breath holding, or body scanning.

SECTION 5

Chapters 4–6: habits, identity, and stress

Chapter 4, Days 16–20: map cue, action, immediate result, and later result. Test an environmental adjustment, a smaller start, an if–then plan, and an observation-based review. Ask: What does the old action provide? What practical barrier needs support? What will the person observe in the next trial?

Chapter 5, Days 21–25: examine tone, values, evidence of strengths, comparison, and flexible identity. Use ordinary strengths rather than exceptional achievements. Ask: What action expresses the value? How can a correction be firm without being insulting? Which part of a comparison is missing context?

Chapter 6, Days 26–30: examine demands and resources, comfortable pauses, rest conditions, influence, and the halfway review. Do not frame adversity as a necessary gift or stress as automatically beneficial. Ask: Which demand needs practical change? What is within influence? Who can help?

Halfway acknowledgment should name effort, learning, or help-seeking without requiring better mood scores. Do not compare learners or infer that the curriculum caused a mood change. Make adjustments to workload, timing, or group format based on actual needs.

SECTION 6

Chapters 7–9: relationships, pace, and setbacks

Chapter 7, Days 31–35: practice boundaries, clear requests, digital purpose, support mapping, and repair. Ask: What action is the person choosing? What power or safety issues affect the conversation? Can an apology and a boundary coexist? Avoid role-playing a threatening or coercive encounter.

Chapter 8, Days 36–40: connect a value to a goal, plan with capacity, define stopping points, rehearse returning after an interruption, and review friction. Ask: What makes the first action possible? What can wait? Which resource is missing? Do not use streaks or public completion rankings.

Chapter 9, Days 41–45: separate a setback from a verdict, use a short debrief, practice accountable kindness, distinguish forgiveness from reconciliation, and build a response plan. Ask: What happened? What contributed? What support or repair is appropriate?

Forgiveness, contact, and reconciliation are never completion requirements. Do not ask participants to revisit traumatic events to demonstrate a skill. Use a mild fictional mistake and allow an unsent draft. Escalate concerns about abuse or danger through approved safeguarding routes.

SECTION 7

Chapters 10–12: future direction and continuation

Chapter 10, Days 46–50: explore possible futures, a near milestone, a future-perspective letter, alternative routes, and a next-month plan. Ask: What value matters across different routes? What requirement needs verifying? What action provides information without creating an unsafe commitment?

Chapter 11, Days 51–55: choose a daily anchor, notice a balanced good moment, build a coping menu, prepare a support request, and rehearse on an ordinary day. Ask: Which option fits which need? What signals that more help is needed? How can the support route be made easier to find?

Chapter 12, Days 56–60: gather evidence of learning, tell a flexible story, prepare a maintenance plan, choose a bounded contribution, and write a continuing manifesto. Ask: What can the learner describe, try, or ask more clearly? What remains unresolved? What is worth carrying forward?

The ending should not portray people as broken before the program or fixed after it. Celebrate voluntary participation and practical learning. Offer a private acknowledgment. Explain exactly what the participation certificate does and does not represent.

SECTION 8

Responding to distress and safeguarding concerns

Prepare before the session: know the organization’s emergency contact route, safeguarding lead, local emergency services, and referral options. Know who takes responsibility if the facilitator needs help. This guide does not replace legal obligations, clinical judgment, or an approved crisis protocol.

If a participant appears distressed, pause the activity, reduce the audience, speak calmly, and offer choices that fit immediate safety. Do not demand a detailed account, investigate an allegation, diagnose, or promise secrecy. Ask only what is needed to connect the person with appropriate support under policy.

If someone may be in immediate danger, contact emergency services and follow the organization’s approved emergency procedure. Do not leave a person unsupported while waiting for a workbook response. In the United States and territories, 988 provides crisis support; online chat is available at https://988lifeline.org/chat/. Elsewhere, use verified local services.

For concerns that are not immediate emergencies, consult the safeguarding lead or qualified professional through the approved route. Document only necessary facts in the authorized secure system. Do not store disclosures in the public workbook. Arrange follow-up responsibility and debrief with appropriate supervision.

SECTION 9

Accessibility, media, and respectful participation

Check keyboard access, readable contrast, text size, screen-reader labels, and the ability to take breaks. The reading-comfort setting increases spacing and uses familiar sans-serif text; it is not a clinically validated dyslexia intervention. Offer alternative formats according to participant needs.

Every lesson includes text and a visual sequence. The website includes an introductory slideshow and browser read-aloud. Browser voices are not approved prerecorded TEF narration, and availability or processing depends on the device. Never tell learners that private entries are part of a server recording. Reading a reflection requires an explicit action.

This release is English. Do not present it as a completed bilingual program. If facilitating in another language, use an appropriately reviewed translation and equivalent examples, instructions, boundaries, and accessible media. Do not rely on a browser translation as proof of educational parity.

Use fictional peer stories as examples, not testimonials. If you later introduce real stories, obtain appropriate permission and privacy review. Avoid selecting participants to represent a community or requiring them to explain an identity, disability, or difficult experience to others.

SECTION 10

Completion, review, and responsible follow-up

Completion means self-reported participation in all sixty lessons. The website allows learners to mark lessons reviewed and undo marks. Writing, mood check-ins, and quiz answers are optional. The participation certificate becomes available after all sixty review marks and is printed locally with a chosen display name.

Certificates do not indicate accredited learning, professional competence, clinical benefit, or recovery. There is no connected email or Google Apps Script certificate service. If an organization adds one later, use reviewed consent, secure records, verified completion rules, duplicate prevention, and explicit delivery-status handling.

For program review, ask about clarity, access, relevance, pacing, and whether learners could exercise choice. Avoid inferring treatment effectiveness from completion rates or mood charts. Use aggregate feedback only when appropriately collected and do not require private journals as evaluation data.

Close with a concrete continuation choice: two useful practices, a boundary, a support route, and a review date. Record any organizational action separately from participant reflections. The curriculum and this guide should be reviewed when needs, policies, or source guidance change.
